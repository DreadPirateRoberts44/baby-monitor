While computers process mathematical frequencies, experienced humans use a completely different dataset: context.
Method	What It Measures	Strengths	Major Weaknesses
Machines (AI/Apps)	Pitch, volume, and the ratio of sound-to-silence.	Excellent at identifying sudden, acute pain.	Easily fooled by background noise; cannot tell "tired" from "hungry".

When it comes to isolating the cry bout, is that true for the training data as well?
Yes, absolutely. You must isolate and chunk the cry bouts in your training data first, otherwise your model will struggle during inference.
If you train a model on long, unsegmented audio files, it will learn the wrong patterns and tank your accuracy.
Why Training on Unsegmented Data Fails
A typical 10-second or 30-second audio clip of a crying baby is actually mostly "dead air" or irrelevant features:
The Anatomy of a Cry: A real cry is a rhythmic loop: Expiratory Blast (the sound) 
 Pause 
 Inspiratory Gasp (catching breath) 
 Pause.
The Data Contamination: If you pass a raw 10-second clip into your training pipeline, the model processes the gasp, the silence, and the background noise alongside the actual cry. It dilutes the unique spectral features (like the formant frequencies or harmonic energy) that actually indicate pain or hunger.
By chunking your training data into uniform 1-second windows, you force the neural network to look strictly at the high-density acoustic properties of the vocal blast itself.
How to Implement This in Your Pipeline
To build a clean training dataset from longer audio files, you should implement an automated pre-processing script before extracting your log-Mel spectrograms:
Voice Activity Detection (VAD) / Energy Thresholding: Do not just blindly chop a 10-second file every 1 second. Use a basic Root Mean Square (RMS) energy threshold (using libraries like librosa or scipy) to find where the baby is actually making noise. Discard the silent gaps or gasps where the energy drops below your threshold.
Fixed-Window Chunking: Take the continuous sections of high-energy crying and slice them into uniform lengths (e.g., 975ms or 1000ms).
Data Augmentation (Bonus): Slicing longer files this way naturally expands your dataset. A single 10-second Kaggle file might yield 5 or 6 highly distinct, clean 1-second training samples.
Inference Matching
In machine learning, your training distribution must match your inference distribution. When your sister eventually uses the app, the microphone will record a continuous stream of audio. Your app's backend will slice that real-time audio stream into the exact same 1-second increments, run them through the model, and average the predictions over a few seconds to give her a final result.

Ambient Room Temperature & Humidity: Babies cry frequently if they are too hot or cold, but they can't regulate their own body temperature well. Sudden drops or spikes in room temperature can be a highly weighted feature for predicting a "discomfort/environmental" cry.

Minutes Since Last Wake-Up (Wake Window): Newborns can typically only handle being awake for 45 to 90 minutes before getting overtired. Your system can build a unique baseline profile for this specific baby over time, mapping out what is normal for her.
Cry Duration & Intensity Velocity: Pain cries start at maximum intensity instantly. Hunger and tiredness usually build up gradually with minor "fussing" bouts before escalating into a full cry. By tracking the rate of volume/pitch escalation over a 3-minute window, you can filter out acute issues from routine needs.
The "Sun-Downing" Profile: Many infants experience a period of intense fussiness in the evening (often called the "witching hour"). Your model can look at the rolling 7-day history to weight "fussiness" higher if the cry occurs between 5:00 PM and 8:00 PM.

Because of this, your second instinct is spot on: you should use a rule-based algorithm to influence the audio prediction outside of a traditional machine learning model. This is incredibly common in production engineering and is much lighter on your Raspberry Pi.
Here is how you can practically structure the hardware, select a lightweight model, and use smart logic overrides to make the app incredibly accurate for your sister.
1. Recommended Hardware Stack (Raspberry Pi Setup)
To keep things simple, cheap, and modular, you can use a Raspberry Pi 4 (2GB or 4GB) or the newer Raspberry Pi 5. The hardware peripherals you need cost only a few dollars each:
Microphone: A simple USB omnidirectional microphone plug-in, or an I2S Microphone Breakout board (like the SPH0645) which wires directly to the Pi's GPIO pins for incredibly clean audio.
Environmental Sensor: A DHT22 (Temperature & Humidity) or a BME280 sensor.
Light Sensor: A simple BH1750 I2C Ambient Light sensor.
Physical Buttons: Two tactile momentary buttons wired to GPIO pins labeled "Fed" and "Changed."
2. The Ultra-Lightweight Audio Model
To run real-time inference on a Raspberry Pi without it overheating or lagging, you want to avoid massive deep learning frameworks.
The Architecture: Use a MobileNetV2 or a very small custom 3-layer 2D CNN.
The Optimization: Once you train your model in PyTorch or TensorFlow, convert it to TensorFlow Lite (TFLite) or ONNX Runtime. This quantizes the model weights from float32 to int8. A TFLite MobileNetV2 will run a 1-second spectrogram inference on a Raspberry Pi in just a few milliseconds, utilizing almost zero CPU.
3. Implementing Rule-Based Post-Processing (The "Sanity Check")
Instead of training a model on tabular data you don't have, let your lightweight TFLite model output its raw probabilities, and then use a standard python if/else script to mathematically alter the results based on your sensors and buttons.
Think of it like adding or subtracting "weight" to the model's confidence scores:
python
# Hypothesized raw output from your Pi's TFLite Audio Model
# format: [Hunger, Pain, Fussy/Tired]
audio_probabilities = [0.45, 0.15, 0.40] 

# Passive metrics collected by your Pi's hardware scripts
minutes_since_fed = 180  # 3 hours
room_temp = 79.5         # Too hot for a baby (ideal is 68-72°F)

# ---- RULE-BASED INFLUENCE LAYER ----

# Rule 1: If it's been more than 2.5 hours, heavily favor hunger
if minutes_since_fed > 150:
    audio_probabilities[0] += 0.25 # Boost Hunger
    audio_probabilities[2] -= 0.10 # Lower Fussiness

# Rule 2: If the room is noticeably too hot/cold, favor discomfort/fussiness
if room_temp > 75.0 or room_temp < 65.0:
    audio_probabilities[2] += 0.20 # Boost Fussy/Discomfort
    audio_probabilities[0] -= 0.10 # Lower Hunger

# Normalize the probabilities back so they equal 1.0 (100%)
final_predictions = normalize(audio_probabilities)
Use code with caution.
By tweaking these rule-based weights using standard newborn developmental milestones (like the 2-to-3-hour feeding window or 60-minute wake windows), you can manually build an exceptionally smart system without needing a single row of tabular training data.
